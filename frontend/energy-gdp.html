There are no rich, low-energy countries

Jonathan Keogh

Filed under “Blog” · 17 July 2026

Take every country with both figures reported, once per year from 2000 to 2023: a panel of 4,728 country-year observations of GDP per capita $G$ and primary energy use per capita $E$—all energy a country consumes, not just the fifth or so delivered as electricity. The title is a statement about the support of their joint law—the region $\{G \text{ large},\, E \text{ small}\}$ carries no mass—and it survives a close look. It is the sharper claim: a rich country must command a great deal of energy, which is why cheap and secure energy supply is not a luxury but a precondition for prosperity.

The fit

Both variables range over three orders of magnitude, so work in logs. Ordinary least squares on the pooled panel gives

$$\log_{10} E \;=\; \alpha + \beta\,\log_{10} G + \varepsilon,\qquad \hat\beta = 0.93,\quad \hat\alpha = 0.56.$$

The relationship is tight and monotone: Pearson $r = 0.89$ ($R^2 = 0.79$), Spearman $\rho = 0.90$, residual scatter $\hat\sigma = 0.33$ dex—a typical country sits within a factor of $10^{0.33} \approx 2.1$ of the line. Since $\hat\beta \approx 1$, energy use is very nearly proportional to income: energy intensity $E/G$ is roughly scale-invariant across the entire development range.

All 24 years at once

Rather than one map per year, bin the whole panel and shade each cell by how many country-years land in it. The mass concentrates on a straight diagonal band; the lower-right is blank.

The other corner: energy without wealth

The empty region is the lower-right; the upper-left is not empty at all, and it says something important. The conspicuous outliers far above the line are petrostates: Turkmenistan (about \$5,300 per head, yet roughly 59,500 kWh of primary energy—some 5× above the fit) and Trinidad & Tobago (about \$18,300 and 109,000 kWh, roughly 3× above).Both sit on abundant domestic gas; consumption is inflated by subsidised prices, flaring, and energy-intensive export industry—LNG, ammonia, petrochemicals. These economies have cheap, secure energy in abundance—and remain middle-income. So the implication runs strictly one way. A great deal of energy is necessary for wealth but not sufficient: energy that is merely extracted and burned, rather than converted into productive capital and diversified output, buys tonnes of oil-equivalent but not much GDP. The empty lower-right is the binding constraint; the populated upper-left is the reminder that clearing it is only the first step.

Between countries vs. within one

A pooled correlation conflates two questions. Split each series into a country mean and a deviation from it, $\log_{10} X_{it} = \bar X_i + \tilde X_{it}$, and measure each piece separately:

  • Between the 200 countries (their long-run means): $r_B = 0.91$. Richer nations use more—the cross-section.
  • Within a country over time (deviations): $r_W = 0.49$, slope $\beta_W = 0.26$. When a country’s own income rises, its energy use rises too, but sub-proportionally—efficiency improves and the mix tilts toward less energy-intensive services.

Both signs agree. The pattern is not an artifact of comparing nations frozen at one instant; it also holds along each country’s own trajectory.

The empty corner

The claim concerns the lower edge of the cloud, not its center. Binning by income and taking the 5th percentile of $E$ (the green wall) yields a lower envelope that is monotone increasing: lift the income floor and the energy floor lifts with it. Sharpening to the corner, of the 772 country-years with $G \ge \$30{,}000$, none has $E$ below 15,000 kWh—the lowest is 17,631 kWh (Macao SAR, China, 2022). Empirically

$$\widehat{\Pr}\bigl(E < 15000\ \text{kWh} \;\big|\; G \ge \$30\text{k}\bigr) \;=\; \frac{0}{772} \;=\; 0.$$

Two caveats

First, country-years are not independent: a country contributes up to 24 near-repeated points, so the effective sample is far below 4,728. The honest content is persistence across 24 annual cross-sections, not 4,728 independent draws. Second, correlation fixes no direction—cheap energy enables output and output funds energy, with common drivers (industrialisation, institutions) behind both.The literature calls this the “energy–growth nexus”; for e.g. see Kraft & Kraft (1978) and Ozturk (2010). Neither caveat touches the support statement, which is what the title asserts: no country has yet been wealthy without consuming a great deal of energy.
