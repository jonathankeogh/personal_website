There does not exist a high-GDP, low-electricity country

Jonathan Keogh

Filed under “Projects”

Take every country with both figures reported, once per year from 2000 to 2023: a panel of 4,710 country-year observations of GDP per capita $G$ and electricity use per capita $E$. The title is a statement about the support of their joint law—the region $\{G \text{ large},\, E \text{ small}\}$ carries no mass—and it survives a close look.

The fit

Both variables range over three orders of magnitude, so work in logs. Ordinary least squares on the pooled panel gives

$$\log_{10} E \;=\; \alpha + \beta\,\log_{10} G + \varepsilon,\qquad \hat\beta = 0.95,\quad \hat\alpha = -0.36.$$

The relationship is tight and monotone: Pearson $r = 0.87$ ($R^2 = 0.76$), Spearman $\rho = 0.91$, residual scatter $\hat\sigma = 0.36$ dex—a typical country sits within a factor of $10^{0.36} \approx 2.3$ of the line. Since $\hat\beta \approx 1$, electricity is very nearly proportional to income: the intensity $E/G$ is roughly scale-invariant across the entire development range.

All 24 years at once

Rather than one map per year, bin the whole panel and shade each cell by how many country-years land in it. The mass concentrates on a straight diagonal band; the lower-right is blank.

Between countries vs. within one

A pooled correlation conflates two questions. Split each series into a country mean and a deviation from it, $\log_{10} X_{it} = \bar X_i + \tilde X_{it}$, and measure each piece separately:

  • Between the 199 countries (their long-run means): $r_B = 0.89$. Richer nations use more—the cross-section.
  • Within a country over time (deviations): $r_W = 0.60$, slope $\beta_W = 0.35$. When a country’s own income rises, its electricity rises too, but sub-proportionally—generating capital is slow to build and rich economies tilt toward services.

Both signs agree. The pattern is not an artifact of comparing nations frozen at one instant; it also holds along each country’s own trajectory.

The empty corner

The claim concerns the lower edge of the cloud, not its center. Binning by income and taking the 5th percentile of $E$ (the green wall) yields a lower envelope that is monotone increasing: lift the income floor and the electricity floor lifts with it. Sharpening to the corner, of the 772 country-years with $G \ge \$30{,}000$, none has $E < 2000$ kWh—the lowest is 3,887 kWh (Cyprus, 2021). Empirically

$$\widehat{\Pr}\bigl(E < 2000\ \text{kWh} \;\big|\; G \ge \$30\text{k}\bigr) \;=\; \frac{0}{772} \;=\; 0.$$

Two caveats

First, country-years are not independent: a country contributes up to 24 near-repeated points, so the effective sample is far below 4,710. The honest content is persistence across 24 annual cross-sections, not 4,710 independent draws. Second, correlation fixes no direction—cheap power enables output and output funds power, with common drivers (industrialisation, institutions) behind both.The literature calls this the “electricity–growth nexus”; the direction of causation genuinely varies by country. Neither caveat touches the support statement, which is what the title asserts: no country has yet been wealthy without consuming a great deal of electricity.
